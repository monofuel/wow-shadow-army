### Question assumptions before iterating

Multiple agents have lost 12+ hours this week by treating guesses as facts and
then stacking fixes on top of them. For traverse work, an assumption is not a
finding. Before changing policy or navigation behavior:

1. Write down the proposed cause and label each supporting statement as either
   **observed**, **inferred**, or **unknown**.
2. Point to the exact trace fields, timestamps, positions, or game behavior that
   support every observed claim. Correlation is not proof of causation.
3. Identify the smallest run or existing log query that could disprove the
   assumption. Prefer disproving it before implementing around it.
4. If the required observation is missing, improve telemetry or capture a
   focused trace. Do not fill the gap with movement heuristics or recovery code.
5. After a change, verify the predicted state transition in the new trace. Code
   compiling, a run lasting longer, or the bot moving somewhere is not proof
   that the hypothesis was correct.
6. If two iterations do not produce the predicted evidence, stop modifying that
   hypothesis and re-audit the observations. Never continue for hours by adding
   compensating thresholds and special cases.

Known bad assumptions that must not silently return:

- Fatigue does not need to remain above 0.80. The successful manual run reached
  0.592 remaining and recovered while continuing along the coastal corridor.
- A guessed water-surface height is not an observed water state. Use ClientView
  liquid and swimming observations, with unknown/stale represented explicitly.
- Travel Form normally cancels through water entry; do not invent a
  `/cancelform` transition.
- Aquatic Form can be lost. When the character remains wet and swimming, confirm
  the loss through ClientView and reapply it once; do not assume it persists,
  spam casts, or jump out of the water.
- Bottomless ocean is swimmable. Modeled seafloor depth is telemetry, not a
  swimming permission gate.
- Open ocean does not have a valid ground navmesh. Use the liquid route and real
  collision observations; do not interpret a ground-navmesh failure as proof
  that the water is impassable.

### Learnings from the manual route

- The manual trajectory is proof of a traversable water corridor. Use its
  positions and observed state as evidence; do not imitate the 1,775 individual
  keypresses.
- Ocean-bottom depth does not determine swimmability. Bottomless ocean remains
  swimmable. The route risk is accumulating fatigue by moving too far offshore,
  not losing permission to swim because the modeled bottom is deep or unknown.
- The successful route stayed near the coastal cliffs without trying to touch
  or collide with them. Real physical collision should reject a heading; a
  guessed shoreline or depth threshold should not.
- The complete run spent about 214 seconds with fatigue active across 16 timer
  intervals. Its lowest recorded remaining fraction was **0.592** near
  `(-5999.77, -4798.39)`, and it finished with the timer inactive/full. This
  disproves the experimental recovery trigger at 0.85 remaining: the human
  route crossed that value repeatedly without needing an emergency retreat.
- The current direct chord toward `wet_leg_a` is not an adequate description of
  the route around the first coastal cliff. The manual trajectory bends around
  that terrain and then continues through a broad fatigue-safe coastal corridor.

### Implementation and validation plan

1. Reduce the successful manual trajectory to a sparse route centerline. Keep
   points at meaningful bends, cliff clearance, fatigue boundary changes, and
   the Dustwallow endpoint; do not create a dense keypress or waypoint replay.
2. Make that centerline the primary long-horizon liquid route. Allow the fork's
   live collision observations to make short local corrections, then rejoin the
   corridor instead of selecting an unrelated heading or repeatedly reversing.
3. Remove modeled depth from liquid movement permission and candidate rejection.
   Retain it as telemetry. Gate movement on observed liquid, swimming, Aquatic
   Form, physical collision, progress, and fatigue.
4. Use fatigue only as a safety layer over the cliff-side route:
   - above 0.50 remaining: follow the route normally;
   - from 0.25 through 0.50: apply a gentle shoreward preference without
     reversing, stopping, or oscillating;
   - below 0.25: deliberately recover toward the most recent observed
     fatigue-safe water;
   - resume normal routing when the timer is clearly replenishing or clears.
5. Compare every automated trace with the manual reference after the run:
   along-route progress, cross-track error, first significant divergence,
   collision/no-progress events, form loss, fatigue minimum and trend, and
   heading reversals. This durable comparison is the normal 10x debugging loop;
   the admin port is not timing-critical.
6. Change only the earliest failure supported by the trace, rerun it, and record
   the run id and evidence. Do not add recovery behavior for an assumed cause.
7. First achieve one clean automated arrival at the manual Dustwallow endpoint,
   then require five consecutive clean arrivals before extending the route
   toward Merchant Bay.
